# Literature Review Summary

This research asks what kinds of architecture a territorial water infrastructure produces, taking the Melen Water Supply Project as its primary case, and focuses on the question of visibility. The literature review considers visibility not simply as a visual condition but in relation to legibility, knowledge, access, representation, and publicness, and it takes as its framework the question of whether this research belongs within visual culture or critical geography. The review draws on the TU Delft Repository, DSpace@MIT and MIT Primo, HOLLIS, JSTOR, Google Scholar, Crossref, OpenMETU, and Academia.edu, and it verifies each reference through Crossref, publisher pages, repository records, or the full text.

Twenty-one of the thirty sources in the review address the question of visibility directly. In terms of approach, nine sources belong to visual culture and eight to critical geography, while thirteen fall within neither field. The review also distinguishes sources that place building, space, landscape, or design at the centre of their analysis from those that approach infrastructure through anthropology, science and technology studies, geography, philosophy, or management without an architectural analysis.

The conceptual axis of the review lies in the tension between Star and Larkin. Star and Ruhleder (1996) list "becomes visible upon breakdown" among the dimensions of infrastructure (p. 113), and Larkin (2013, p. 336) identifies Star (1999) as the source of the assertion that infrastructures are "by definition invisible". Larkin questions this assertion and argues that invisibility "is only one and at the extreme edge of a range of visibilities that move from unseen to grand spectacles and everything in between" (p. 336). This research can define the question of visibility in relation to this range.

Foucault (1977) and Deleuze (1988) provide the theoretical vocabulary that links architecture, visibility, and power, Deleuze through the distinction between "the visible and the articulable" (p. 32). Kaika and Swyngedouw (2000) and Kaika (2006) offer the architectural argument closest to the question of this research. Kaika and Swyngedouw read dams, purification plants, and pumping stations as icons that early modernity designed, ornamented, and placed prominently in the city, and they trace how these structures "began to disappear from the cityscape" in high modernity (Abstract). Gandy (2014), Easterling (2014), Colven (2020), Mattern (2013), and Kambic (2015) inform the registers of representation, access, and publicness, while Kılıç (2020) and Sarıkaya Işık (2019) offer comparable material on the water infrastructure and water structures of Istanbul.

The visual culture sources concentrate on representation, whereas the critical geography sources address visibility together with access, knowledge, and publicness, and they do so for water. The registers of knowledge, access, and publicness, which this research locates in the protection plan, in the fence and the service road, and in the filtration strips, find their closest counterparts in the critical geography sources, and both groups share the register of representation. On this basis, critical geography can cover a wider range of the registers of visibility for territorial water infrastructure, while the visual culture sources remain the reference for representation and for the theoretical vocabulary of visibility. The sources point towards the following formulation: "This research is situated at the intersection of architectural theory, infrastructure studies, and critical geography, examining how architecture mediates the visibility, legibility, and public intelligibility of territorial water infrastructure." Several sources approach architecture through history, which leaves open whether the first field reads architectural theory or architectural history and theory.

Full texts or verified pages support the reading of five sources. For the others, including most of the critical geography sources, the reading rests on abstracts, publisher descriptions, or titles, and it remains open to revision once this research reads their full texts. This research has not yet read Varnelis (2008) and the two works by Mathur and da Cunha (2001, 2016) for the question of visibility. Murphy (2024) is a course description of the MIT Department of Architecture rather than a publication. The placement of each source within visual culture or critical geography is a reading that rests on verified statements, not a position the sources themselves declare.
