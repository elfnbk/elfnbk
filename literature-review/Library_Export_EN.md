# Making Visible and Keeping Concealed: Architectures of Melen Water Supply Project

Elif Bek

## Abstract

This research asks what kinds of architecture a territorial water infrastructure produces. It examines this question through the Melen Water Supply Project, a system that supplies drinking water to Istanbul and that operates while the reservoir giving the project its name has not yet been impounded. Only a small part of the interventions carried out in the basin is accessible as information. This research therefore focuses on the question of visibility: how does architecture make an otherwise largely invisible water infrastructure visible, legible, and publicly imaginable, and, conversely, how does it conceal, obscure, or restrict access to it?

The architectural structures and spaces that constitute the Melen system will be examined with consideration of visibility not simply as a visual condition but in relation to legibility, knowledge, access, representation, and publicness. These structures that define the relationship with water also define how interaction with water gave shape to the built environment across supply, sanitation, and flood mitigation alike (Murphy, 2024).

Each register within the Melen Water Supply Project can be approached through a different set of elements that contain the issue of visibility. Legibility can be read in the structures that are passed without being recognised for what they are, the intake regulator and the pumping station among them. It can also be read in the treatment plants, which are recognisable as a building type but not as part of a project that supplies another city. Knowledge can be traced in what is documented and released, since much of the architecture appears publicly only through a protection plan and its annexed map, including the interception collectors that run buried alongside the watercourses and the dispersed network of wastewater facilities. Access can be observed in the fence, the gate, the service road, and the distance the regulations prescribe between recreational use and the water intake. Representation can be located in the drawing, the photograph, and the caption through which this dual system of supply and sanitation can be defined in its entirety. Publicness, understood here as visibility in relation to public use, can be discussed in elements such as the filtration strips, bands of land along the watercourses that the administration expropriates for İSKİ, grades, and plants so that vegetation filters surface runoff before it reaches the water.

The questions of how architecture mediates between the technical infrastructure of water and what becomes visible, to whom, and through what architectural forms and spatial arrangements will be posed. The Melen Water Supply Project comprises structures that are approached and used, as well as structures that are passed without notice at the "infra" as the spaces of water that become visible and invisible intermittently within the basin. By setting the visible and the invisible of the project's architecture, and the relationalities between them will illustrate the stance on the interaction of water within the city.

## Literature Review

The literature review conducted within this research was built on core readings that address the visibility of infrastructure and on the question of whether the research is positioned within visual culture or critical geography, taking visibility in relation to legibility, knowledge, access, representation, and publicness as its framework. The TU Delft Repository, DSpace@MIT and MIT Primo, HOLLIS, JSTOR, Google Scholar, Crossref, OpenMETU and Academia.edu were searched with a focus on architecture-focused sources in both fields and on sources that address the question of visibility, and each reference was verified through Crossref, publisher pages, repository records, or the full text.

Of the thirty sources included in this review, twenty-one address the question of visibility. The remaining nine comprise four architecture-focused sources in visual culture, one architecture-focused source in critical geography, one other source with a strong architectural approach, two sources with a weak architectural approach and one master's thesis.

### Sources addressing the question of visibility

Atas, Z., Atmaca, Y., & Tasguzen Polat, Z. (2026). Historic water infrastructure as an urban-spatial system in the old city of Mardin: A multi-scalar spatial-decoding approach. *Urban Science*, *10*(8), 428. https://doi.org/10.3390/urbansci10080428

Relevance to this research: According to the publisher's abstract, in the old city of Mardin "the transition to centralized modern supply" transformed "the visibility, accessibility, use, and situated knowledge of water", and the study reconstructs the relationships "between partially concealed infrastructure and visible urban form".

Architectural approach: strong.

Verified link: https://doi.org/10.3390/urbansci10080428 (verified through Crossref, MIT Primo and HOLLIS (Harvard))

Atkinson, T. (2025). *Monumental designs: Infrastructure and the culture of the Tennessee Valley Authority*. University Press of Mississippi. https://doi.org/10.2307/jj.25465122

Relevance to this research: The first chapter, "A Dam Site More Modern: Developing TVA Monumentality in Visual Culture" (pp. 24-56), situates the infrastructure of the Tennessee Valley Authority within visual culture. The register of representation can be located in this material.

Architectural approach: strong.

Verified link: https://doi.org/10.2307/jj.25465122 (verified through JSTOR search result and Crossref)

Biersteker, E., Koppenjan, J., & van Marrewijk, A. (2021). Translating the invisible: Governing underground utilities in the Amsterdam airport Schiphol terminal project. *International Journal of Project Management*, *39*(6), 581–593. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijproman.2021.04.003

Relevance to this research: According to the abstract, the article draws on "translation theory, which is essentially about the reinterpretation, appropriation, and representation of interests related to materials", and shows that "the translation of the underground by project actors hampered the necessary relocation of utilities". The register of knowledge can be traced in this account of underground utilities.

Architectural approach: weak.

Verified link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijproman.2021.04.003 (verified through Crossref and TU Delft Repository)

Bowker, G. C., & Star, S. L. (1999). *Sorting things out: Classification and its consequences*. MIT Press. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/6352.001.0001

Relevance to this research: According to the publisher's abstract, the authors "emphasize the role of invisibility in the process by which classification orders human interaction", examine "how categories are made and kept invisible, and how people can change this invisibility when necessary", and treat systems of classification "as part of the built information environment". The register of knowledge can be traced through this account of classification.

Architectural approach: weak.

Verified link: https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/6352.001.0001 (verified through Crossref)

Colven, E. (2020). Subterranean infrastructures in a sinking city: The politics of visibility in Jakarta. *Critical Asian Studies*, *52*(3), 311–331. https://doi.org/10.1080/14672715.2020.1793210

Relevance to this research: According to the publisher's abstract, the article explores "the politics of infrastructure in Jakarta through the lens of in/visibility", notes that infrastructure studies has tended to categorise infrastructure "as either hyper-visible by design, or invisible until breakdown", and argues that "spectacular, visible infrastructures generate public and political attention, while below ground, hidden and invisible infrastructures are overlooked". The register of publicness can be discussed through this "politics of visibility".

Architectural approach: weak.

Verified link: https://doi.org/10.1080/14672715.2020.1793210 (verified through Crossref)

da Cunha, D. (2019). *The invention of rivers: Alexander's eye and Ganga's descent*. University of Pennsylvania Press.

Relevance to this research: According to the publisher's description, rivers are treated as "products of human design and a particular way of seeing that has roots stretching as far back as ancient Greek cartography", and the book traces how the Ganges "has been pictured, mapped, surveyed, explored, and measured across the millennia"; its first chapter is titled "Alexander's Eye and Ganga's Descent". The register of representation can be discussed through this way of seeing.

Architectural approach: strong.

Verified link: https://www.pennpress.org/9780812249996/the-invention-of-rivers/ (verified through Penn Press publisher page and review in Planning Perspectives)

Deleuze, G. (1988). *Foucault* (S. Hand, Trans.). University of Minnesota Press. (Original work published 1986)

Relevance to this research: On p. 32, Deleuze writes that the prison "constitutes a visibility" and is "a system of light before being a figure of stone", and introduces Foucault's distinction between "the visible and the articulable". The register of knowledge in this research can be discussed through this distinction.

Architectural approach: weak.

Verified link: https://archive.org/details/foucault0000dele/page/32/mode/2up?q=%22the+visible+and+the+articulable%22 (verified through Internet Archive search inside (University of Minnesota Press 1988))

Easterling, K. (2014). *Extrastatecraft: The power of infrastructure space*. Verso.

Relevance to this research: According to the publisher's description, "infrastructure sets the invisible rules that govern the spaces of our everyday lives", and the book reveals governmental and corporate forces "buried within the concrete and fiber-optics of our modern habitat". The register of knowledge can be discussed in relation to these "invisible rules" of infrastructure space.

Architectural approach: strong.

Verified link: https://www.versobooks.com/products/30-extrastatecraft (verified through Verso publisher page)

Foucault, M. (1977). *Discipline and punish: The birth of the prison* (A. Sheridan, Trans.). Pantheon Books. (Original work published 1975)

Relevance to this research: On p. 200, Bentham's Panopticon is described as "the architectural figure of this composition", followed by the sentence "Visibility is a trap." On p. 201, the major effect of the Panopticon is "to induce in the inmate a state of conscious and permanent visibility that assures the automatic functioning of power." The relation between architecture, visibility and power in this research can be traced to these pages.

Architectural approach: strong.

Positioning: field, Philosophy; axis, Visual culture (the text's own field is disciplinary power); basis, p. 200: the Panopticon as "the architectural figure of this composition"; "Visibility is a trap." p. 201: "conscious and permanent visibility". The axis placement is not declared by the source itself but is a reading based on verified statements.

Gandy, M. (2014). *The fabric of space: Water, modernity, and the urban imagination*. MIT Press. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/8313.001.0001

Relevance to this research: According to the publisher's abstract, water lies "at the intersection of landscape and infrastructure, crossing between visible and invisible domains of urban space"; the book begins with "the Parisian sewers of the nineteenth century, captured in the photographs of Nadar" and addresses Berlin's "protection of public access to lakes for swimming". The registers of representation and access can be read in these cases.

Architectural approach: strong.

Verified link: https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/8313.001.0001 (verified through Crossref)

Hutton, J. (2019). *Reciprocal landscapes: Stories of material movements*. Routledge. https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315737102

Relevance to this research: The publisher's description opens with the question: "How are the far-away, invisible landscapes where materials come from related to the highly visible, urban landscapes where those same materials are installed?" The book traces five construction materials "from seminal public landscapes in New York City, back to where they came from".

Architectural approach: strong.

Verified link: https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315737102 (verified through Crossref)

Kaika, M., & Swyngedouw, E. (2000). Fetishizing the modern city: The phantasmagoria of urban technological networks. *International Journal of Urban and Regional Research*, *24*(1), 120–138. https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-2427.00239

Relevance to this research: According to the publisher's abstract, "Burying the flow of water via subterranean and often distant pinpointed technological mediations (dams, purification plants, pumping stations) facilitates and contributes to masking the social relations through which the metabolic urbanization of water takes place"; in early modernity such structures "were celebrated as glorious icons, carefully designed, ornamented and prominently located in the city", whereas in high modernity they "began to disappear from the cityscape" and "from the urban imagination".

Architectural approach: strong.

Verified link: https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-2427.00239 (verified through Crossref)

Kaika, M. (2006). Dams as symbols of modernization: The urbanization of nature between geographical imagination and materiality. *Annals of the Association of American Geographers*, *96*(2), 276–301. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8306.2006.00478.x

Relevance to this research: According to the publisher's abstract, the article analyses "the iconography and symbolism of dam constructions" through the Marathon dam, which "became an iconic marker of Athens's modernization" and was "veneered with the same marble as that used for the Parthenon", read as an effort "to reconcile iconographically the two prevailing geographical imaginations" of the city. The register of representation can be located in this reading of the dam as architecture.

Architectural approach: strong.

Relevance to this research: According to the chapter abstract, Kambic examines "three responses to inaccessible water infrastructure" in Los Angeles, namely "accepting the situation, incremental infrastructure, and the Cyborg City", under the title of urban water visibility, legibility and access.
