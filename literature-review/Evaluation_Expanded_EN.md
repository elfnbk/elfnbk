--- Değerlendirme metni ---

Introduction

This review evaluates thirty sources on the relation between architecture, infrastructure and visibility. The sources were identified through the TU Delft Repository, DSpace@MIT and MIT Primo, HOLLIS, JSTOR, Google Scholar, Crossref, OpenMETU and Academia.edu, and each reference was verified through Crossref, publisher pages, repository records or the full text. They were classified along three lines:

* whether they address the question of visibility, which applies to twenty-one of them;
* whether their approach can be placed within visual culture or critical geography, which groups nine and eight sources respectively, the remaining thirteen being assigned to neither field;
* whether their architectural approach is strong or weak.

Each source was read for the registers in which visibility can be traced, namely legibility, knowledge, access, representation, and publicness, and for the level of evidence on which this reading rests. Full texts or verified pages were available for eight sources. The evaluation of the others rests on abstracts, publisher descriptions or titles, and can be revised when their full texts are read.

Stage 1: Delimiting the fields of this research

The formulation under discussion situates this research at the intersection of architectural theory, infrastructure studies, and visual culture, examining how architecture mediates the visibility, legibility, and public intelligibility of territorial water infrastructure. The sources reviewed can be read against each of these fields in turn.

Infrastructure studies can be named as a field on the basis of the sources themselves. Colven (2020) refers to infrastructure studies directly and notes that the field has tended to categorise infrastructure "as either hyper-visible by design, or invisible until breakdown" (Abstract). The second of these positions can be traced to Star and Ruhleder (1996), for whom "the normally invisible quality of working infrastructure becomes visible when it breaks" (p. 113). It can also be traced to Star (1999), whose assertion that infrastructures are "by definition invisible" Larkin (2013, p. 336) questions. Larkin proposes instead "to examine how (in)visibility is mobilized and why" (p. 336), and the question of visibility in this research can be defined in relation to the range of visibilities he describes. Easterling (2014) similarly notes that infrastructure "is considered to be a hidden substrate," and argues that it is now "the overt point of contact and access between us all" (p. 11). Bowker and Star (1999) extend the role of invisibility to classification, and Graham and Marvin (2001) to the interactions between infrastructure networks and urban spaces.

The architectural side of the formulation is supported by a smaller group of sources in which visibility is tied to built form:

* Foucault (1977) describes the Panopticon as "the architectural figure of this composition" and states that "visibility is a trap" (p. 200).
* Kaika and Swyngedouw (2000) observe that dams, purification plants and pumping stations were, in early modernity, "celebrated as glorious icons, carefully designed, ornamented and prominently located in the city," and that in high modernity they "began to disappear from the cityscape" (Abstract).
* Demirtaş (2000) analyses "water dams and reservoirs that are more than utilitarian structures" (Abstract).
* Ghosn and Jazairy (2018) describe architectural drawing as "a device that brings the Earth into matters of concern; it makes visible absent things" (p. 22).
* Kılıç (2020) addresses the design of the urban water infrastructure of Istanbul and argues that the "'Modern Water' understanding of today" makes water "invisible to users" (Abstract).

The remaining terms of the formulation also find support in the sources:

* Legibility: Kambic (2015) examines "three responses to inaccessible water infrastructure" (Abstract) under the title of urban water visibility, legibility and access. Mattern (2013) discusses tours, audio guides and artistic projects that address what seems "illegible or even un-mappable" (Touring, Collecting, Documenting Infrastructure section, para. 3). She also cautions that "the ambitious intentions to 'make visible the invisible'" can "too often become ends in themselves" (From Perception to Awareness … and Action section, para. 2).
* Publicness: Colven (2020) argues that "spectacular, visible infrastructures generate public and political attention" (Abstract).

Stage 2: Visual culture or critical geography

The grouping of the sources into visual culture and critical geography is that of this review. Atkinson (2025) names visual culture as the setting of its first chapter, whereas none of the sources grouped under critical geography names that field. They are grouped through the geographical imagination and the urbanization of nature and water that they address (Kaika, 2006; Kaika & Swyngedouw, 2000). The two groups can be compared through the registers in which they address visibility.

The visual culture sources concentrate on representation:

* Atkinson (2025) situates the dams of the Tennessee Valley Authority within visual culture in the chapter "A Dam Site More Modern: Developing TVA Monumentality in Visual Culture."
* Da Cunha (2019) traces how the Ganges has been pictured, mapped and surveyed.
* Sarıkaya Işık (2019) examines the Aqueduct of Valens as depicted heritage.
* Demirtaş (2000) addresses the experience of water infrastructure as urban and natural space.

Mattern (2013) and Ghosn and Jazairy (2018) extend this group towards legibility and publicness. Ghosn and Jazairy do so through aesthetic assemblies that recall the "devices of wonder that first presented Nature to publics" (p. 25). The theoretical vocabulary of visibility can also be read on this side. Foucault (1977) is one source of it. So is Deleuze (1988), for whom the prison "constitutes a visibility" and is "a system of light before being a figure of stone" (p. 32). Mitchell (2002) maps visual studies as "an emergent academic formation" (Abstract). Three sources in this group, Mathur and da Cunha (2001, 2016) and Varnelis (2008), have not yet been read for the question of visibility.

The critical geography sources address visibility together with access, knowledge and publicness, and they do so for water:

* Kaika and Swyngedouw (2000) argue that burying the flow of water through "subterranean and often distant pinpointed technological mediations (dams, purification plants, pumping stations)" contributes to "masking the social relations" through which the urbanization of water takes place (Abstract).
* Kaika (2006) reads "the iconography and symbolism of dam constructions" through the Marathon dam of Athens (Abstract), which places the register of representation within this group as well.
* Gandy (2014) situates water at "the intersection of landscape and infrastructure, crossing between visible and invisible domains of urban space" (p. 1). He describes the Paris sewers photographed by Nadar as the setting for "an intriguing encounter between the new optical preoccupations of modernity and the latest advances in engineering science" (p. vii), and traces the widening of public access to the lakes of Berlin (pp. 64, 70).
* Atas et al. (2026) show that the transition to centralised supply transformed "the visibility, accessibility, use, and situated knowledge of water" (Abstract).
* Hutton (2019) relates the distant landscapes where construction materials come from to the urban landscapes where they are installed.
* Colven (2020) frames the question as a politics of visibility, and Kambic (2015) frames it as one of legibility and access.

The Melen Water Supply Project can be read against this comparison. Its intake regulator, pumping station and treatment plants supply another city from a basin in which only a small part of the interventions is accessible as information. The registers of knowledge, access and publicness, which this research locates in the protection plan, the fence and the service road, and the filtration strips, find their closest counterparts in the critical geography sources. The register of representation is shared by both groups.

On the basis of the sources reviewed, critical geography can cover a wider range of the registers of visibility for territorial water infrastructure than visual culture, which concentrates on representation. The formulation can accordingly read: "This research is situated at the intersection of architectural theory, infrastructure studies, and critical geography, examining how architecture mediates the visibility, legibility, and public intelligibility of territorial water infrastructure." In this formulation, the visual culture sources remain the reference for the register of representation and for the theoretical vocabulary of visibility. For most of the critical geography sources this evaluation rests on abstracts and publisher descriptions, and it can be revisited once their full texts are read.
